House leader says South China Sea code’s progress still laudable
MANILA, Philippines — Even if the proposed Code of Conduct (COC) in the South China Sea remains unfinished, a ranking House of Representatives lawmaker believes the current administration still deserves praise for speeding up the process — from 50 percent completion to 80 percent in a year’s time.
In a statement on Monday, Deputy Speaker Paolo Ortega V said President Ferdinand Marcos Jr.’s administration can be credited with its “sustained diplomatic engagement,” which accelerated discussions on the proposed COC.
Ortega’s remarks came after Foreign Affairs Secretary Maria Theresa Lazaro admitted on Sunday that while the COC is now at around 80 percent, finishing it will now be in Singapore hands as the next chairperson of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (Asean).
“From roughly 50 percent to nearly 80 percent provisional agreement in a single year. That is the kind of progress that speaks for itself. Under President Marcos, the Philippines has shown that determined diplomacy, backed by a clear sense of national interest, can move even the most complicated negotiations forward,” Ortega said.
“Look at where we started and where we are today. After more than two decades of discussions, we are now approaching the final stretch of an agreement that could shape peace and stability in the South China Sea for generations. The Philippines helped bring us to this point, and that is something every Filipino can take pride in,” he added.
Asean member-states and China agreed in 2023 to accelerate negotiations and finalize the COC by 2026 — which coincides with the Asean chairmanship of the Philippines, one of the countries with overlapping claims over the South China Sea.
The proposed agreement would have established rules to manage maritime disputes and prevent confrontations over the South China Sea. Previously, Malacañang said that part of the goals laid out by Marcos for the Philippines’ Asean chairmanship is the finalization of the COC, as it is intended to help de-escalate tensions in the contested waters.

In a press briefing on Sunday at the International Media Center for the 49th ASEAN summit, Lazaro pointed to negotiations and the consideration of several countries’ legal frameworks — as there may be a need for respective congresses to ratify the deal — as the reasons why the COC was not finalized.
Lazaro said that if only the Philippines had five to six extra months, the COC would have been finished while the country chaired Asean.
Despite the setback, Lazaro said negotiations are entering the “final phase,” and that the Philippines’ stewardship is moving the COC quickly. She also noted that the COC was only about 50 percent completed when the Philippines took charge of Asean, but it has moved to 80 percent in a few months’ time — therefore being one of the administration’s achievements.

Ortega said that it is hard to get the consensus of all 11 Asean member-states — including China — which is why crafting such an agreement takes time and needs patience from different parties.
“Getting 11 countries to agree on the rules that will govern one of the world’s most strategically important waterways takes patience, persistence and considerable diplomatic skill. President Marcos and our negotiating team have demonstrated all three,” Ortega said.
“Singapore will be working with a draft that is substantially more advanced because of the Philippines’ efforts. That is how Asean cooperation should work. Each chairmanship builds on the achievements of the previous one, and together we move closer to a common objective,” he added.
Discussions on the COC come amid rising tensions between the Philippines and China, due to disputes over the West Philippine Sea (WPS) and statements from the Chinese Embassy in Manila and Philippine lawmakers.
Last October, some members of the Philippine House of Representatives called out Chinese Ambassador to the Philippines Jing Quan for saying that while China can easily win in a military contest, it seeks a peaceful resolution to the dispute.
Ortega and other members of the House’s Young Guns bloc fired back, saying that true peace will not be attained if one side makes threats.

Due to this squabble, former Surigao del Norte Rep. Robert Ace Barbers and Mamamayang Liberal Party-list Rep. Leila de Lima separately warned the Philippines about the possibility of China suddenly playing negotiator when ASEAN member-states convene for the 49th summit.
Ortega said that the important thing is that the Philippines is clear on its stand about international law and maritime rights.
“Ang mahalaga, malinaw ang ating paninindigan. We are pursuing an agreement that respects international law, protects our maritime rights and promotes peace in the region. Those principles have guided President Marcos throughout these negotiations, and they will continue to guide the Philippines moving forward,” he said.
(What matters is that our position is clear. We are pursuing an agreement that respects international law, protects our maritime rights and promotes peace in the region. Those principles have guided President Marcos throughout these negotiations, and they will continue to guide the Philippines moving forward.)
“Our position is anchored on international law, and that gives us a strong foundation in every discussion. We want a Code of Conduct that countries will respect, that will help prevent dangerous encounters at sea and that will allow our fishermen and maritime personnel to exercise their lawful rights safely,” he added.
In 2016, the Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA) awarded the Philippines exclusive rights over its waters, including the WPS as China’s nine-dash line claim lacks legal or historical basis. This means the WPS, a body of water that forms part of the South China Sea, belongs to the Philippines because it sits within the country’s exclusive economic zone.

China, however, has insisted on ownership of many islands inside the WPS, despite the award.
Several incidents between the two countries’ water assets have been well-documented: just this July, the Armed Forces of the Philippines reported that a Navy personnel member was injured after members of the Chinese Coast Guard (CCG) assaulted him during a confrontation at Ayungin Shoal.
In August 2023, a CCG vessel water-cannoned Philippine vessels that were only trying to bring supplies to soldiers at BRP Sierra Madre.

Finishing South China Sea COC now in Singapore’s hands – Lazaro
MANILA, Philippines — Finishing the Code of Conduct (COC) in the South China Sea will now be in Singapore’s hands, as Philippine authorities lacked the time needed to wrap up several issues, Foreign Affairs Secretary Maria Theresa Lazaro said on Sunday.
Despite this, Lazaro said in a press briefing at the International Media Center for the 49th Association of Southeast Asian Nations (Asean) Summit that the Philippines considers the progress in the COC one of the successes that came out of the country’s hosting of the biannual tilt.
However, with only 80 percent of the COC finalized, the code may only be finished during Singapore’s turn as the Asean chair in 2027.
“The parties agreed to continue to work constructively with a view to concluding the negotiations on an effective and substantive code of conduct at the earliest possible time — and actually this means that we give it up, we leave this almost 80 percent finished document to Singapore as the next chair,” Lazaro said.
“I have to emphasize that it is one already of the achievements of the Philippines as chair, because what was the COC before we shepherded it? It was only about 50 percent, perhaps? But to go to 80 and even moving, then it’s a little more discussions when it’s in Singapore, but indeed, this Code of Conduct is really something that we can be proud of,” she added.
Asean members and China agreed in 2023 to accelerate negotiations and finalize the COC by 2026. The proposed agreement would have established rules to manage maritime disputes and prevent confrontations over the South China Sea, a heavily disputed area, as some Southeast Asian countries, including the Philippines, have overlapping claims with China and its neighbors.
The Philippines has maintained that the COC should be consistent with international law, particularly the 1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (Unclos), and it should not undermine the rights of countries not parties to the agreement.
Previously, Malacañang said that part of the goals laid out by President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. is the finalization of the COC. In a briefing last Nov. 9, the Palace press officer, Undersecretary Claire Castro, said sealing the agreement has long been one of the president’s goals, as it is intended to help deescalate tensions in the contested waters.

Asked why the member states failed to finalize the COC under the Philippines’ leadership of the Asean, Lazaro pointed to negotiations and the consideration of several countries’ legal frameworks — as there may be a need for respective congresses to ratify the deal.

If only the Philippines had five to six extra months, Lazaro believes the COC would have been finished while the country chaired Asean.
“Well, negotiations are negotiations are negotiations. In other words, the ten Asean countries plus China have also, of course, different views on how things are. So that is the reason why, I think there was not enough time. If we were given probably another five to six months, I think we can finish that,” she said.
“But these are very… some have their sensitivities, like if you say that the document is legally binding, there is also a ratification process on the part of certain Asean member states. So I guess there are certain issues akin or rather which is to each and every Asena member-state. And I’m not only talking about ten Asean member-states (there is also) China,” she added.
Despite the seeming setback, Lazaro said negotiations are entering the “final phase,” and that it is through the Philippines’ stewardship that the COC is moving fast.
“The negotiators now enter their final phase, and we commend the dedication and constructive engagement of all delegations. In particular, the co-chairs of the Code of Conduct is Malaysia and China, and we commend them for their able stewardship of the complex process,” the Department of Foreign Affairs head said.
“It may be [that] the co-chairs [are] Malaysia and China, but it is the Philippines as chair who has been shepherding the whole process. Their efforts have advanced the early conclusion of an effective and substantive COC, consistent with Unclos,” she noted.
The negotiations come amid rising tensions between the Philippines and China, due to disputes over the West Philippine Sea (WPS) and statements from the Chinese Embassy in Manila and Philippine lawmakers.
Last October, some members of the Philippine House of Representatives called out Chinese Ambassador to the Philippines Jing Quan for saying that while China can easily win in a military contest, it seeks a peaceful resolution to the dispute.
According to members of the House’s Young Guns bloc, true peace will not be attained if one side makes threats, saying that Jing’s statements carry a threat, not an assurance of peace.

Due to this squabble, former Surigao del Norte Rep. Robert Ace Barbers and Mamamayang Liberal Party-list Rep. Leila de Lima separately warned the Philippines about the possibility of China suddenly playing negotiator when Asean member states convene for the 49th summit.
In 2016, the Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA) ruled in its award that the Philippines has exclusive rights over its waters, including the West Philippine Sea, as China’s nine-dash line claim lacks legal or historical basis. This means that the WPS, a body of water that forms part of the South China Sea, belongs to the Philippines as it sits inside the country’s exclusive economic zone.

China has insisted, however, on ownership of many islands inside the WPS, despite the award.
Philippine-Chinese ties recovered during the administration of President Rodrigo Duterte, who once called the PCA award a piece of paper that can be thrown in the waste bin. But since Marcos assumed office in 2022, relations have become strained again, as his administration insisted that the Philippines would not cede an inch of its territory to foreign powers.
Several incidents between the two countries’ water assets have been well-documented: just this July, the Armed Forces of the Philippines reported that a Navy man was injured after members of the Chinese Coast Guard (CCG) assaulted him during a confrontation at Ayungin Shoal.
In August 2023, a CCG vessel water-cannoned Philippine vessels, which were only trying to bring supplies to soldiers at BRP Sierra Madre.
